Short answer: no. Bra sizes are not universal. The same size label can fit differently from one brand to another, and sizing labels can change between countries. A size such as 34C is useful shorthand, but it does not describe every detail of the bra or guarantee that two bras with that label will fit alike.

This is why a size that feels comfortable in one bra may feel too tight, too loose, too shallow, or too full in another. It does not automatically mean your body or measurements have changed. The garment and the sizing system may be different.

Why bra sizes are not universal

1. Brands do not all build bras to the same measurements

A size label is attached to a manufacturer's pattern and sizing chart. Brands can make different choices about band stretch, cup depth, wire width, coverage, and the shape of the cups. Two bras marked with the same size can therefore have different dimensions and feel different when worn.

This is not only a matter of personal preference. A 2025 study compared bras from seven manufacturers using mannequin and breast-sizer measurements. It found significant differences in cup volume between manufacturers across the sizes tested, with greater variation at larger cup sizes. The study covered a limited set of brands and sizes, so it should not be read as a measurement of every bra on the market. It does, however, show why a shared label should not be treated as a guarantee of identical cup volume.

2. Countries use different sizing conventions

US, UK, EU, and Australian size labels are not one interchangeable scale. Band labels are expressed differently in some systems, and cup-letter sequences can diverge as sizes increase. For example, US and UK systems do not use the same sequence after D in many common charts.

There is no single conversion table that accurately represents every brand. Some manufacturers publish their own international conversion charts, and those charts should take priority when shopping for that brand.

3. A cup letter depends on the band

A cup letter is not a fixed volume by itself. It is used in combination with a band size within a particular sizing system. A 30D and a 36D do not represent the same cup capacity simply because both use the letter D.

This is also why sister sizing changes both parts of the label. A nearby sister size may offer a similar cup capacity with a different band, but it is not an identical replacement. The bra's proportions and how it sits can change.

4. Bra design changes how a size feels

Even within one brand, different styles can fit differently. A molded T-shirt bra, an unlined seamed bra, a wireless bra, and a sports bra are built for different purposes. Their cup shapes, stretch, coverage, and support structures are not necessarily alike.

Two styles in the same labeled size may therefore feel different. The size label is only one part of the comparison; the shape and construction matter too.

What research says about sizing differences

Research has examined both the limits of bra-size standardization and the differences between measurement-based sizing and professional fit assessment.

  • Manufacturer variation: Moscarelli and colleagues (2025) measured bras from seven manufacturers and reported significant differences in cup volume among the tested products. The study is useful evidence of variation, but its sample does not cover every manufacturer, size, or style.
  • Standardization challenges: Bengtson and Glicksman (2015) reviewed the problem of standardizing bra cup sizes and discussed why cup labels are not a simple universal measure.
  • Measurement methods: White and Scurr (2012) compared traditional measurement-based sizing with professional fitting criteria and found differences between the approaches in their study sample. A calculated label should therefore be treated as a starting point, not a fit verdict.

Does the same size mean the same thing in every country?

No. Regional systems use different band and cup conventions. A conversion chart can help translate a label, but the conversion is a guide rather than a promise that the resulting bra will fit exactly the same.

When buying an international brand, use the brand's own chart for the country and product you are ordering. Do not assume that a size converter's general mapping overrides a manufacturer's current guidance.

Why you may wear different sizes across brands

It is common for a shopper to compare more than one size while trying different brands or styles. Several garment details can explain the difference:

  • Band tension: bands can vary in firmness and stretch.
  • Cup shape: cups can be wider, narrower, deeper, shallower, or more projected.
  • Wire shape: wire width and curve can change where the bra sits.
  • Fabric: firm, stretchy, lined, and molded materials behave differently.
  • Coverage: plunge, balconette, full-cup, and other cuts cover the body differently.

These are garment differences, not a reason to keep changing your measurements until every brand produces the same label.

Example: one label, two different bras

Imagine two bras are both labeled 34C. One has a firm band, a shallow molded cup, and a wide wire. The other has a stretchier band, a deeper seamed cup, and a narrower wire. The labels match, but the bras are not built the same way. One may feel comfortable while the other does not.

This example illustrates why a label cannot describe every fit detail. It is not a claim that all molded cups or all seamed cups fit in one particular way; construction varies by product.

Are sister sizes universal alternatives?

Sister sizing is a useful way to identify nearby size labels that are intended to have similar cup capacity. When the band goes down, the cup letter generally moves up; when the band goes up, the cup letter generally moves down. The exact label progression depends on the sizing system.

Similar cup capacity does not mean identical fit. A different band changes the way the bra sits around the body, and the cups' proportions can also change. Read Sister Sizing Explained before treating a sister size as a direct substitute.

The bottom line

Bra sizes are not universal across brands, countries, or styles. A shared label does not guarantee the same measurements, cup volume, or fit. Use measurements to find a starting point, check the chart for the exact bra, and judge the garment by how it fits. Measurements are a starting point, not a verdict.
