Direct answer

A bra size combines a band label and a cup label. A calculator can estimate a starting label by rounding an underbust measurement and comparing bust with underbust. The exact rules differ across sizing systems, and manufacturer labels do not guarantee identical garment fit. Use the result as a reference, then check the chart and fit of the bra you are considering.

Two parts of a size label

The band number is a label associated with the measurement around the body below the bust under a particular sizing convention. The cup letter marks a position in that system's cup sequence. Neither part fully describes the shape or construction of a finished bra.

Band Starting Point

Measure the underbust according to the instructions for the sizing system being used, then apply that system's band convention. Avoid presenting a single rounding rule as universal because commercial size charts differ.

Cup Starting Point

A simple method is to compare the full-bust and underbust measurements. This site uses approximately one cup step per inch of difference for its basic calculator. That is a starting convention, not a universal industry standard.

Why Fit Checking Matters

Research comparing tape-measure sizing with professional fit criteria found significant differences between the two approaches. The researchers recommended putting more emphasis on fit criteria and less on treating a calculated size as absolute. (White & Scurr 2012, Ergonomics)

Why There Is No Single Universal Bra-Size Formula

Bra sizing looks mathematical because it uses a number and a letter, but the label is only a shorthand for a garment size. Different countries and manufacturers use different conventions, and the finished bra can behave differently depending on its fabric, construction and shape.

That is why two calculators can give different answers from the same measurements without one necessarily being “broken.” They may simply be applying different sizing conventions.

A Worked Example

Imagine a snug underbust measurement of 32 inches and a standing bust measurement of 36 inches. The four-inch difference becomes the cup index in the simple method used by Bra Size, giving a D-cup starting point in the US/UK sequence.

The calculation is deliberately easy to understand: first establish the band starting point, then compare bust and underbust to select a cup index. The result is a useful place to begin shopping—not a guarantee that a 32D from every manufacturer will fit the same way.

What Can Change the Result in Practice?

  • Measurement technique: a tape that is too loose or tilted can change the result.
  • Sizing system: US, UK, EU and AU labels do not use identical cup progressions.
  • Garment construction: molded, stretch, seamed and wireless bras can fit very differently.
  • Body shape: two people with the same measurements can need different cup shapes.

From a Number to a Real Bra

Once you have a starting size, try the bra with the band, cups and straps adjusted normally. If something feels wrong, diagnose the fit before assuming the entire calculation was useless. Our bra-fit checklist and Bra Fit Checker can help with that next step.

How we calculated this

Band starting point: round snug underbust to the nearest whole inch; if the rounded value is odd, move to the next even number.

Cup starting point: subtract snug underbust from standing bust, round the difference to the nearest whole inch, and use the result as an index in the selected cup sequence. Negative differences are treated as zero.

Regional labels: the site maps the index to its stored US, UK, EU, or AU cup sequence. These sequences are calculator mappings, not a universal manufacturer's chart.

Why methods can produce different answers

Some sizing methods add inches to the underbust before selecting a band label; BraSizePRO's main calculator does not. The important distinction for this site is transparency: the formula is shown so that you can reproduce its result. The calculation alone cannot establish which bra will fit best. A study comparing traditional measurement sizing with professional fit criteria found significant differences between the methods in its sample. White & Scurr (2012).

Why cup labels are not universal

US, UK, EU, and AU systems do not always use the same cup progression. In addition, a 2025 study comparing bras from seven manufacturers found variation in cup volume between brands across the sizes tested. The study's sample does not cover every brand, size, or style, but it supports a clear caution: a shared label does not guarantee identical cup volume. Read Moscarelli et al. (2025).

What to do with your starting size

  1. Take measurements consistently using the at-home measuring guide.
  2. Use the Bra Size Calculator to reproduce the site's starting label.
  3. Check the manufacturer's current chart for the exact product.
  4. Assess the band, cups, wires, center front, and straps together; do not rely on one fit sign alone.
